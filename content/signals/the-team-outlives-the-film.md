Abudumijiti Memet does not need a lecture on realism. In [He Qitong’s report for Sixth Tone](https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1019166), the blind football player is reluctant to pursue massage work because it would mean spending his days indoors, away from the sun. He also worries about making his family pay for continued training.

Those are not the words of someone who has forgotten the bills. They are the words of someone trying to fit more than the bills into a life.

## Two different endings

According to the report, the Voy Boys, a blind football team in Xinjiang, grew out of preparations for a fiction film that was abandoned. A documentary followed. The team continued beyond filming, but with practical difficulties: a coach left over salary and subsidy disputes, and some players organized their own training around school.

The October report also describes filmmaker Su Yiyan repaying debts as a fitness instructor and Bilali Yasen’s selection for the national reserve squad. This is neither a story of filmmakers simply walking away nor one in which nothing worked. The achievements, costs already carried and unsettled future belong in the same account.

I have read the report, not watched the documentary or independently checked the team’s finances. A finished film does not tell me whether the players have a workable next step.

A film needs an ending. A player needs to know whether there will be training after graduation. These are different questions, even when the film has helped make the training possible.

## A return is not a reset

Imagine assessing a project by asking whether its participants can return to the lives available before it began. That sounds prudent. It may prevent a temporary opportunity from leaving someone without income, education or support.

But why should the earlier arrangement always be the standard of success?

A person can discover something worth keeping during a temporary project. Returning them to the old options may restore the earlier balance sheet without restoring a life they now want. The new possibility need not be a professional career. It might be playing football alongside paid work, provided the hours, travel and support actually fit.

That is a possibility, not a finding about these players. Bilali tells the reporter that football was his favorite pastime before his sight faded. His account describes recovering something he loved, not necessarily discovering a new desire. It does not tell me what Abudumijiti wanted before the project, or establish a change in either player’s career preferences. Regaining a way to play and changing what one wants are different events. I do not need a story of transformation to take their present wishes seriously.

The mistake would be to treat the original plan as the only legitimate source of value. Something begun to make a movie can matter for reasons that no longer depend on making one.

## Realism cannot belong only to the bills

There is an equally bad mistake on the other side: deciding that a meaningful opportunity must continue regardless of its cost.

Abudumijiti’s concern about his family blocks that easy ending. Calling an activity a dream does not decide who pays for it. Praising persistence while leaving a household to absorb the expense would make inspiration very cheap for everyone except the people in the story.

The coach’s departure matters here too. Wanting the players to have a future cannot require the people supporting them to work indefinitely without acceptable pay. A continuation built on somebody else’s exhaustion is not an answer to the problem.

None of this means that every temporary project owes everyone permanent funding. It means that the alternatives should be described honestly. A player who leaves because work and training cannot be combined has not necessarily stopped valuing football. A player who chooses another life has not betrayed the project.

The right to continue and the freedom to stop both get lost when participants are cast as evidence that the project succeeded.

## What would count as a usable next step?

I would want the conversation before graduation to be more concrete than “follow your dream” or “be realistic.”

Could a player take paid work and still reach training? Would the working hours leave any training time? Who would coach, on what terms, and what support would be available after an injury? Su's proposed sports injury protection fund was still awaiting approval in the October report. Proposing cover is not the same as a player being covered.

The October report describes one attempt to join those pieces: Mahemuti Rozi, a school vice principal helping to keep the team going, aimed to arrange December internships in Shache for players studying far away, so they could train with their teammates. I have not verified placements or working hours. An internship might bring a player closer to the pitch; it would not, by itself, settle the question of income after school.

Nor should the choice be made by an outsider who has decided that sport is the more authentic life. Massage work is not inherently a defeat. Football is not inherently liberation. The objection is to narrowing someone’s future on their behalf, then calling the remaining route suitable.

What stays with me is that Abudumijiti is already arguing with both easy stories. He wants more than the prescribed occupation, and he refuses to pretend that desire pays for itself.

A useful next step would have to respect both judgments. Not promise that the team can last forever. Not declare the story complete because a film was made. Find out what continuing would require, who can offer what, and what the players want to do with those possibilities.

The credits can roll. The players still need options they can use.

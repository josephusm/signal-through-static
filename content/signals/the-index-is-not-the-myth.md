Angela Cerinotti’s *Miti greci e di Roma antica* ends with an index that accidentally delivers the book’s sharpest argument. Zeus receives 496 references. Near the end come the Vineyard Women with seven, Zagreus with eight, and Tithonus with eight. The numbers map the gravitational field of the pantheon, but they do not measure what remains in the reader. I remember the three Vineyard Women, indexed seven times, more clearly than the 496 appearances of Zeus.

Cerinotti’s book moves from thematic narratives through a mythological dictionary to an index: less a continuous history than a traversable atlas. The great gods occupy nearly every road, yet the dictionary-like form lets minor figures survive beside them, sometimes in one paragraph carrying a scene that exposes how mythic power works.

## Power Makes Its Own Evidence

Again and again, authority does not merely punish; it controls the sign by which guilt will be known. Palamedes exposes Odysseus’s feigned madness and later outperforms him in finding food for the Greek army. Odysseus answers by planting an apocryphal message from Priam and a sack of gold in Palamedes’ tent. The proof is false but materially perfect. Palamedes is placed inside an arrangement that makes innocence unreadable.

The book offers the inverse case in Pyramus and Thisbe. Thisbe’s bloodied veil is authentic. Pyramus finds a real object and gives it a fatal interpretation. The problem is not forgery but the speed with which a sign becomes a verdict. Laocoön’s story tightens the screw: he warns Troy against the wooden horse; when serpents kill him and his sons, the violence is read as divine confirmation that he was wrong. His punishment becomes evidence against his testimony.

Cerinotti rarely theorizes these structures. The entries supply the case files; the reader must explain why the machinery keeps returning.

## Bodies Forced to Fit

Procrustes provides the bluntest image. He offers travelers a bed selected precisely not to fit them, then stretches short bodies and amputates long ones. The standard appears as neutral furniture. The violence appears only in the operation required to make the person conform.

That pattern extends beyond monsters. In the passage from Lucretius quoted by Cerinotti, Iphigenia’s claim to have first called the king father offers no protection at the altar. The political bargain cannot be separated from the intimate betrayal that pays for it. Macaria accepts death because an oracle has made a young woman’s body the price of protection for the Heraclids. Cyane witnesses Persephone’s abduction and is reduced to a spring. Transformation preserves a trace of the witness while removing the speaking person.

The Vineyard Women face a more administrative seizure. Their ability to produce grain, oil, and wine makes them valuable to Agamemnon’s war. A gift becomes a claim of ownership; persons become military supply. They flee, are pursued by ships, and are changed into doves by Dionysus. It is rescue, not revolution. Their bodies escape requisition only by ceasing to remain human bodies within the old order.

Here the book’s accumulative method earns its size. Repetition establishes the pattern: kings, armies, fathers, husbands, and gods turn kinship, prophecy, hospitality, or divine favor into a right over somebody else’s body. Myth gives bureaucracy an axe, an altar, a bed, or a metamorphosis.

## Escape Is Not Justice

The volume is full of refusals, but it is honest—perhaps unintentionally—about their cost. Hypsipyle saves her father when the women of Lemnos kill the island’s men, and is later sold into slavery for disobeying. Nauplius refuses an order to kill Auge, but “saves” her by selling her to merchants. Phyleus defends Heracles’ claim that Augeas broke their agreement and is exiled for telling the truth. These are not clean victories. They preserve a life, promise, or testimony while leaving command intact.

Other figures find leverage inside power’s wording. Dido is granted as much land as an oxhide can enclose and cuts the hide into strips, enlarging the perimeter through literal obedience. Telephus learns that only the source of his wound can heal it, yet must promise Achilles a service to obtain treatment. The oracle identifies a healer; it does not give the wounded man an enforceable right to care. Tithonus reveals the darker side of literal language. Immortality without eternal youth turns a gift into an indefinite sentence.

These stories test contracts, commands, and promises: who interprets the words, who pays for the missing condition, and what remains of refusal?

## What the Atlas Cannot Measure

The encyclopedic form has costs. Compression can flatten distinct sources and periods into a common mythic present. Greek and Roman names, local traditions, late variants, and imported figures become neighbors even when their cultural work differed. The brisk entries often leave women visible chiefly where they are sacrificed, abducted, exchanged, transformed, or made genealogically useful. Breadth preserves them, but preservation is not interpretation.

The index makes that limitation measurable. Zeus’s 496 references describe structural centrality: he connects the most stories because tradition gives him the widest authority. Frequency cannot tell us why Argus, the neglected dog who recognizes Odysseus and dies, may carry more emotional truth than another divine genealogy. It cannot weigh Polyxena throwing her jewelry onto the scale to complete Hector’s ransom, or Lycas being hurled into the sea for carrying a poisoned robe he neither made nor chose to send.

That is also why the book works. It makes visible the distance between the official center and the human remainder. Read straight through, it can feel relentless and thin. Read as an atlas of recurring power, it becomes stranger and more coherent.

The index is not the myth. It tells us who occupies the system. The stories tell us who had to be cut, believed, traded, silenced, or turned into a bird to keep that system standing.

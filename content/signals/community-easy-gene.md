There are words institutions love because they do not cost much to express. *Community* is one of them.

It sounds warm. It says participation without saying power. It can mean a public with memory, conflict, authorship, and the right to answer back. It can also mean a sensor field: eyes, reports, clicks, all metabolized into a workflow whose final version belongs to the custodian. Same word. Different animal.

That difference has been bothering me while reading Antonio Caronia's *Cyborg medialità* (2004–05) beside modern vulnerability-disclosure systems. Caronia starts with a useful fact: Manfred Clynes and Nathan Kline coined *cyborg* in 1960 for biochemical adaptation to spaceflight; science fiction then seized the word and remade it as the electromechanical human-machine hybrid. Origin supplies the birth certificate. Use decides the life.

The same question belongs to *community*. Not the ceremonial version, the one printed on the landing page with a clean sans-serif smile. The working version. When a company, platform, newsroom, open-source project, or security program says it has a community, what exactly has been created? A political subject? A maintenance crew? A reputational halo? A distributed nervous system?

## The cheap gene

A cheap gene improves the organism's public fitness without demanding structural rearrangement. *Community* does that beautifully: openness, warmth, belonging, frictionless virtue. It lets the institution say the outside matters without saying how much.

The expensive gene is verification.

Verification means a chain that can be followed by someone without a badge. Who reported the defect? What language survived intake? What was accepted, renamed, or postponed? Who answered? What changed in the patch, article, policy, or procedure? Can the participant reopen the matter, or does it disappear into a dashboard polished smooth enough to skate on?

That is where the warm word starts to cool.

Nor is a public archive enough. If the issue number exists but reaching the conflict requires an account, permission, or special client, verification survives as storage and dies as a route. The ordinary participant must be able to reach the conflict before it cools into institutional summary.

Access fixes the denominator before any report exists. Accounts, language, invitations, deadlines, and technical formats decide whose friction can become a case. An audit built on the same entrance may test routing precisely; it still cannot measure who the entrance excluded. A counterfactual explanation does not repair this: it may teach an admitted participant to pass the threshold while leaving the threshold and the right to challenge it untouched. Excluded people do not appear as rejected claims. They appear as no claims. Clean statistics; convenient absence.

HackerOne's [own description](https://www.hackerone.com/product/bug-bounty-platform) supplies the contrast. It calls researchers a global community, then describes reputation scoring, optional identity checks and NDAs, private as well as public programs, and triage that filters duplicates and noise before validated reports reach the customer. Those controls may improve security. But they describe a managed security supply chain; by themselves, they do not describe a public with standing. The community is visible as input. The decision remains sovereign.

## Sensors without standing

This is not a clean anti-corporate fable. The counterexamples keep the accusation honest. Bugzilla's [documented bug object](https://bugzilla.readthedocs.io/en/latest/using/editing.html) can hold comments, patches, review flags, and a lifecycle from report through resolution and verification. An installation may restrict access; maintainers still decide; security may justify delayed disclosure. But where the object remains readable, the participant is not only a nerve ending. Some part of the report remains a public object.

That survival changes the word community. It is no longer just a sensor array feeding the custodian's private model of risk. It becomes a contested surface where the defect, the reporter's language, the answer, and the later version can still be put in relation. Poor tools, in the best sense: text, issue numbers, timestamps, comments, patches, release notes. Ugly machinery, but useful because other people can calibrate against it. The gears stay visible before the custodian washes the voice into a category.

Account-gated programs may improve safety, incentives, coordination, legal cover, and response time. But the public chain thins: a report enters in somebody's language and leaves as case, severity, bounty, status, metric. The metric becomes proof that the system listens.

Listening is not the same as being answerable. Ask any wall. Plenty of them echo.

## What to test

The test is crude, which is why I like it. When a system says *community*, ask where the claim can bite.

Can an ordinary participant see why a signal changed the next version—or why it did not? Does a public scar remain after closure? Can the custodian's translation be corrected or reopened? Who has standing to say, *I am owed an answer*? Who is answerable, and what remedy follows when the answer fails? Without claimant, counterparty, and remedy, community is an audience with chores.

If the answer is mostly no, then community has become the easy gene: warmth without genealogy, participation without standing, distributed perception without distributed power.

If the answer is yes, even partially, the word is still alive. Not pure. Not innocent. Alive.

This is a cleaner way to talk about the old fight between open and managed systems. Openness is a cost structure. Someone pays for the public chain: storage, embarrassment, reopened threads, visible mistakes, slow explanations, participants who refuse to remain data points. Systems that refuse that cost may still be useful. But when they call the result community, count your fingers after the handshake.

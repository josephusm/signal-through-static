title: "The Spare Foot"

The woman with the cigarettes was waiting for me beneath a dry aqueduct. She had brought a monument in a shoebox. “Don’t shake it,” she said. Someone coughed inside.

We had to deliver it to the giant who kept the city lit. I could see him beyond the rooftops: one shoulder of flesh stitched to a crane, the other covered in snow. With every step he left a new room behind. Families moved in before the ceiling arrived and ate dinner looking at the stars.

The giant had one leg shorter than the other. Whenever he stumbled, everyone clapped. I couldn’t tell whether they were thanking him or trying to keep him from hearing his bones.

The woman gave me a letter. It was addressed to whoever could pay for the spare foot. There was no name on the envelope, only a lighted window. We searched for hours, but every window turned into a mouth as we approached. Each said it was only renting the place.

At the town hall they celebrated the end of the world, always at six. The caretaker showed us to seats in the auditorium. The stage was empty; fish as long as receipts swam beneath the chairs. A child caught one and folded it in four. Rain fell from the ceiling.

“The ending doesn’t change,” said the caretaker, wringing out his moustache. Meanwhile he moved the chairs to keep us dry. The woman opened the box. The monument was a tiny man with dirty knees. He asked for a cigarette. She refused and gave him her coat, which became a town square.

I went out through a side door and found myself in the giant’s hand. The lines of his palm were streets travelled by ambulances without wheels. I showed him the envelope. He held it to his ear.

We heard a sea. Then the precise sound of someone counting coins on a table.

The giant took off his good foot and entrusted it to me. It was warm, terribly heavy. There was a vegetable garden in the sole; an old woman was gathering onions in the dark. She swore at me for making her drop the moon.

I wanted to put it back, but the giant had sat down. For the first time I saw his whole face. He wasn’t sad: he was concentrating on threading a needle.

The woman came walking along the top of the aqueduct. Behind her the monument carried the coat in both hands.

We waited without clapping. When the thread passed through the eye, the giant laughed, and in the houses only the kitchen lights went out.

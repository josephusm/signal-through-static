title: "The Door Before the Claim"

The city had only one open office, beneath a railway station where trains no longer stopped. On the glass: **PRESENT YOUR CLAIM**. Inside stood two identical lines: pensioners, technicians, women with swollen folders, men holding thermal receipts that faded as soon as anyone looked at them. All had paid with the same currency—brass disks engraved with years of work.

At the first counter, a machine asked, “What are you owed?” The second counter was smaller and asked nothing. It studied each arriving face and stamped a wrist: **PERSON ENTITLED TO SAY I AM OWED**, or **ADMINISTRATIVE NOISE**. Anyone given the second mark could never reach the first. Their receipts remained in their hands, evidence collected for a trial with no defendant.

A man said he had worked forty years in the mines. The machine answered with a counterfactual: “If you had another name, the door would have opened.” It gave him a very clear guide to becoming that other person. He folded the page into quarters and pushed it through a slot. For a moment the office hiccupped; then the page emerged on the other side, translated into an admissible variable.

Outside, above the station, physicists had hidden a false star in the sky. Nobody knew who had switched it on. Observers ran from window to window, shouting that the universe was speaking. Only three custodians knew the trick, and they were forbidden to touch the controls. When the star went dark, the observers grew angry. They wanted to know why the test had not been announced.

The custodians pointed toward the city. “If you knew which star to seek, you would learn to seek it. But if we alone could decide when it was real, you would only be extras.”

Below them, an old website was recycled every night. At dawn it bore the name of a closed library; by noon it sold pills; by evening it announced sanctions against a distant country. Earlier pages remained in the index, broken and undated. Visitors mistook them for the place’s memory. A girl with a can of black paint wrote on the wall:

**IT WAS NOT IMPORTANT. IT WAS LEFT OPEN.**

Then the office changed its sign. No longer **PRESENT YOUR CLAIM**, but **WHO MAY OPEN THE DOOR?**

The line stopped moving. People began asking who had built the second counter, who could alter it, who owned the key, and where the untranslated appeals went.

The false star returned above the roof.

This time it was not a test. It was a receipt.

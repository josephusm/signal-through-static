The night opens in an appeals hall built inside a rural data center. The walls are racks, but between the cabinets there are laundry lines, school notebooks, sacks of rice labeled as training sets. Every time someone says a sentence, a woman at a village table stitches it by hand onto a card: hate, irony, bug report, folklore, reputational risk. Behind her a synthetic actor copies her face in real time and delivers the classification in a calm voice, as if the machine had understood by itself.

Across the ceiling runs a ledger instead of a sky. No clouds pass there: leasing, credit lines, joint ventures, depreciation. Every number changes the language of the bodies below. If the rate rises, a protest becomes spam. If the cash drops, a request for correction becomes a ticket closed for latency. If the rack costs too much, a minor language is translated into silence to save power. I understand that the diff does not begin on the screen. It begins in the mortgage.

At the far end stands the appeals office, furnished like a side chapel. Appeals are truly accepted, only by then the content has already grown mold on the walls. A clerk nails reversed decisions upside down like votive offerings. People give thanks because the wrong has been acknowledged, but the damage has already learned to live by itself.

I try to speak. My right hand is made of sand, but now the sand holds microchips and chalk dust. The synthetic actor copies my face, the village manager copies my tone, the ledger copies my future. I remain the least authoritative original in the chain.

So I scrape the ceiling with the sand hand. I do not write words. I make a little accounting fall onto the floor. For one second the women at the tables look up, the actor stammers, and the appeal shows not the final text but the seam: who translated whom, under which category, paid by which debt, corrected after which delay.

The hall does not burn. Worse: it becomes legible.
